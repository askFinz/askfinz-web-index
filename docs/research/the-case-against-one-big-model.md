# The case against one big model

The argument is not that more models are better. It is that being structurally unable to use anything else is a liability disguised as simplicity.

## Key points

### The case against one big model

The argument here is narrower than the title. It is not that more models are better, or that every question needs a committee.

### Simplicity is a real benefit and we should say so

One provider, one interface, one set of behaviours to learn. Fewer moving parts, fewer failure modes, one bill, one place to look when something is wrong.

### The monoculture comparison, and where it breaks

The analogy we keep returning to is agricultural. Industrial monocultures are extraordinarily productive until a blight arrives, and the productivity and the fragility are the same property, not two properties in tension.

### Capability is not one dimension

There is an assumption smuggled inside the phrase "the best model": that capability is a single axis, and the best model is best along all of it.

### What we rejected: the two obvious ways to use several models

Having decided against one model, there are two standard ways to use more than one, and we rejected both.

### What variety actually buys

The obvious one is insulation. When your workflow depends on a single provider, that provider's deprecations, price changes and outages become yours, on their schedule.

### Where this position costs us

Variety is a real burden. More options means more surface to explain, more inconsistency in tone across a session, and more ways for a user to be confused about why two similar questions behaved differently.

### What we would do differently

We designed a system that chooses well and then, later, added the ability for a person to disagree with it. That order encodes an assumption we do not actually hold — that the choice is ours to make and the user's to accept.
